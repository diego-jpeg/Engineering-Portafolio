The idea

Over ten weeks of Control Systems Design we studied dynamic systems, whose state changes over time, and the ways to steer them toward a wanted behavior. For the final project I built a control system of my own: a model airplane on a rocker that can only roll, with ailerons that must hold it level when a stream of air pushes on it.

Sense

An inclination sensor reports the roll angle of the model.

Decide

A PID controller on an ESP32 compares that angle with 0 degrees.

Act

A servo moves both ailerons in opposite directions.

The model

The body is a square prism of 3 mm MDF, 4 cm on each side and 30 cm long. Two wings of 20 cm each and 5 cm wide sit at the middle of its top face. They are hollow, with ribs cut in the shape of a wing profile and covered in paper, so they stay light.

The ailerons run along the wings and are made of MDF for stiffness. Levers connect both of them to one 9 g servo at the center of the plane. The servo can move 45 degrees each way and is set so the two ailerons always move in opposite directions. The body is held by a base, and a bearing at each contact point lets it rotate freely.

Electronics

An ESP32 microcontroller reads the sensor, runs the controller and drives the servo. The inclination sensor is a SCA60C, which covers plus or minus 90 degrees and has three pins: supply, ground and output. Its output voltage scales linearly between 0 V and the supply voltage, so the angle follows directly from the voltage.

The ESP32 can supply 3.3 V and 5 V but only accepts 3.3 V logic. For that reason the sensor runs from 3.3 V, which guarantees its output never exceeds the safe limit. The board stays connected to a laptop, which gives it power and receives live data. Since the model never has to move, I used no battery and no wireless link.

The physics

A wing makes lift because of a pressure difference. Its shape makes the air going over the top travel a slightly longer path, so it moves faster than the air below. By Bernoulli’s principle, faster flow means lower pressure, so the pressure above the wing is lower than below and a net upward force appears. The angle of attack adds to this by pushing air downward, and the air pushes back up.

My model has only ailerons, which sit on the trailing edges of the wings and control roll. Raising an aileron reduces the wing’s curvature and its lift on that side, while lowering one increases curvature and lift. Moving them in opposite directions makes one wing lift more than the other, and the plane rotates about its long axis. This roll is also the first step of a turn, since part of the lift becomes a sideways force.

The controller

A PID controller regulates a system by comparing the wanted value with the measured one, computing the error and producing a correction. The proportional part reacts to the present error, the integral part removes error that has built up over time, and the derivative part anticipates the trend and smooths the response. Here the wanted value is 0 degrees, the measurement is the sensor’s angle, and the servo is the plant.

aileron command = (Kp + Ki/s + Kd*s) * error(s)
How the gains can be derived

For roll control the three gains follow from the physics of the model:

Kp = Ix * wn^2 / lx
Ki = Kp / Ti
Kd = 2 * zeta * Ix * wn / lx
  • Ix: roll inertia, the sum of the wings, body and electronics.
  • wn: natural frequency, which depends on the air density, airspeed, aileron area and lift coefficient, the arm lx, and the inertia.
  • Ti: integral time fraction.
  • lx: distance from the center of gravity to the center of an aileron.

In theory, knowing every variable gives ideal gains. In practice the inertia and natural frequency cannot be found exactly without a deeper analysis, so the gains are starting values to tune on the real model.
