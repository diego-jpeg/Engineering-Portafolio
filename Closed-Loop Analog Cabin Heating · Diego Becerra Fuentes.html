01 · ConceptWhat is closed-loop control?

Closed-loop control means a system measures its own output and uses that measurement to decide what to do next. A thermostat is the everyday example: it senses the room temperature, compares it with a target, and switches the heater until the two agree. An open-loop version would run the heater on a timer without ever checking the result.

Analog electronics can build the same idea entirely from continuous voltages. A sensor such as the LM35 turns temperature into a voltage, op-amps shift and scale that signal, and a comparator with hysteresis (a Schmitt trigger) checks it against a reference and makes the on/off decision. A driver stage then turns that decision into enough current to run the actuator, here a fan. Because the fan changes the very temperature the sensor reads, the loop closes by itself with no code, and the hysteresis keeps it from flicking on and off around the setpoint.

02 · ContextThe challenge

John Deere México asked for a system that mimics heating a tractor cabin with engine heat, using thermofluid principles and analog control, validated on a scaled-down prototype. We built it as a team: two small MDF cabins separated by a gap that holds the fan, with a 40 W heater standing in for the engine.

When the cabin cools, the fan pushes warm air across. Once the cabin is in range the fan switches off, so the loop closes without any microcontroller. I took part in designing, building and testing both the circuit and the cabin.

03 · ElectronicsHow the controller works

The LM35 reads cabin temperature as 10 mV per °C, and an RC low-pass filter cleans up the noise. An inverting summer with a −12 V bias then shifts the signal into the comparator’s range, a non-inverting Schmitt comparator with a +2.2 V reference sets the on and off thresholds, and an inverting stage with gain −11 feeds a power driver that supplies the fan’s current.

The math behind each stage

Sensor      V_LM35 = 10 mV/°C · T

Stage 1     Vo1 = −Rf1 · (V_LM35/R_LM + V_off/R_off)
            Rf1 = 2.2 kΩ, R_LM = 1 kΩ, R_off = 136.4 kΩ, V_off = −12 V
            Vo1 = −2.2·V_LM35 + 0.1936
            at 36.2 °C (0.362 V) → Vo1 ≈ −0.603 V
Stage 2     β = Rf2 / (Rf2 + Rg) = 2.2 / 3.3 = 2/3,  Vref = +2.2 V
            V_TH(H) = Vref + β(V_OH − Vref) ≈ +8.73 V
            V_TH(L) = Vref + β(V_OL − Vref) ≈ −7.27 V   (V_OH/OL ≈ ±12 V)

Stage 3     V_fan = −(110 kΩ / 10 kΩ) · Vo2 = −11 · Vo2
Tuning the hysteresis to about 1–2 °C is what turned the fan from chattering into calm, reliable switching.

04 · ThermofluidsResistance network and energy balance

Before building, we simulated three insulation options for the cabin walls. The winner on our goals was foam, which became the basis for the hand calculations.

Heat flow through the walls

Ignoring radiation, heat leaves through four resistances in series: inside convection, the MDF, the foam, and outside convection.

R = 1/(h_in·A) + L_MDF/(k_MDF·A) + L_foam/(k_foam·A) + 1/(h_out·A)

A = 0.039 m²      k_foam = 0.0263 W/mK, L = 1 mm
k_MDF = 0.13 W/mK, L = 5 mm
h_in = 7.53 W/m²K   h_out = 4.3 W/m²K

R = 3.41 + 0.99 + 0.97 + 5.96 ≈ 11.33 K/W

In practice, each watt of heating should lift the cabin roughly 11 K above the outside air.

Convection coefficients

Inside the cabin, the fan’s 10 m³/h airflow is laminar (Re ≈ 2180 on the vertical walls and 1635 on the horizontal ones), and Nusselt numbers of 27.65 and 23.95 give an area-weighted h of 7.53 W/m²K. On the outside, the Grashof and Rayleigh numbers with the Churchill–Chu correlation give 4.74 W/m²K, close to the simulated 4.3.

How much insulation do we need?

For the insulation sizing we used a design heat flow of 5 W through the walls, with 20 °C outside and 36 °C inside at steady state, so the whole wall must have R = 16 K / 5 W = 3.2 K/W.

R_conv = 3.405 + 5.96 = 9.36 K/W      R_MDF = 0.99 K/W
R_foam = 3.2 − 0.99 − 9.36 = −7.15 K/W   →   L_foam ≈ −7.3 mm
A negative thickness means the MDF and the air films already resist more heat flow than the balance requires, so the cabin doesn’t strictly need foam to reach temperature. The takeaway: the weaker the convection, the more the insulation matters.

05 · TakeawaysWhat went wrong, and what I learned

We soldered the circuit before validating it on the protoboard, which made every bug harder to find, and we skipped the datasheets: a wrong LM35 pin order gave bad readings and a fan that never started, and some potentiometers burned out because we hadn’t checked their voltage ratings. With a faulty sensor, we simulated the LM35 with a bench supply, confirmed the control logic, then repeated the test with the real part. Above all, getting electronics and thermofluids to work together took several attempts on both the box and the circuit, and it taught me more than either module alone.

06 · ConclusionWhy simple analog control still matters

A controller built from a sensor, three op-amps and a driver stage has no firmware to crash, reacts instantly, costs very little and fails in ways that are easy to trace. That is why analog loops are still everywhere in industry around the world: thermostats and HVAC systems, industrial ovens and process heaters, motor speed and current limiting, voltage regulation in power supplies, vehicle climate and cooling-fan control, and the protection circuits that shut equipment down when something drifts out of range. Even where microcontrollers do the heavy lifting, the same feedback logic sits underneath, and understanding it at the circuit level makes any control system easier to design and debug.

This was the first project where I combined electronics, CAD design and construction, and simulation in a single system. I simulated the circuit and the heat flow before building anything, designed and built the cabin, then wired the controller and tested the two together until the fan switched exactly when the maths said it should. Seeing the simulation, the model and the physical prototype agree, and tracing the gaps when they didn’t, is the habit I most want to carry into my career as an engineer.
